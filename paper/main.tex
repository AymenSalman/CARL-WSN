% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs) deployed in IoT-driven smart city
environments carry heterogeneous application traffic with fundamentally
different quality-of-service requirements. Existing hybrid routing
protocols switch between proactive and reactive modes based on a single
network metric, making them insensitive to application urgency and
unable to simultaneously optimise energy consumption and end-to-end
latency across different traffic classes. This paper proposes
CARHy-WSN, a Context-Aware Hybrid Routing protocol that introduces a
three-dimensional context vector --- comprising application urgency
class, normalised residual energy, and link stability score --- to
dynamically select the optimal routing mode per packet. CARHy-WSN is
implemented in MATLAB and evaluated against five baseline protocols
under three traffic classes and a mixed-traffic scenario in a 100-node
heterogeneous WSN simulation. Results demonstrate that CARHy-WSN
achieves an 82.5\% reduction in Class~A emergency packet latency
compared to AODV, a 70.2\% reduction in routing overhead compared to
DSDV, and maintains a packet delivery ratio of 100\% across all evaluated
scenarios, while preserving competitive energy balance
throughout all tested configurations. A lightweight decision tree variant achieves 100\% classification
accuracy with only 0.3\,KB memory overhead, enabling
self-configuring deployment. CARHy-WSN prioritises simultaneous
latency and overhead optimisation for safety-critical heterogeneous
IoT deployments, achieving these gains within a deliberate
network lifetime trade-off relative to lifetime-optimised baselines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{W}{ireless} Sensor Networks (WSNs) have emerged as a
foundational component of Internet of Things (IoT)-driven smart city
infrastructure, enabling continuous and distributed monitoring across
domains including environmental sensing, smart transportation,
industrial automation, and emergency response~\cite{salman2021, akyildiz2002}.
As smart city deployments scale in both node density and application
diversity, the routing protocols governing data delivery face
increasing pressure to simultaneously satisfy conflicting requirements:
energy efficiency, low latency, reliability, and adaptability to
dynamic network conditions.

Existing routing approaches broadly fall into three categories.
Proactive protocols, such as DSDV~\cite{perkins1994} and
OLSR~\cite{clausen2003}, maintain routing tables continuously,
delivering low latency at the cost of high energy overhead from
periodic updates. Reactive protocols, such as AODV~\cite{perkins1999} and
DSR~\cite{johnson1996}, discover routes on demand, conserving
energy in sparse networks but introducing significant route
discovery delays that are intolerable
for time-critical applications. Hybrid protocols, such as
ZRP~\cite{haas2001}, partition the network into zones and apply
different modes within and across zone boundaries. However, ZRP and
its variants rely on a fixed zone radius parameter and remain
insensitive to the nature of the traffic being routed.

In a realistic smart city WSN, a single node may simultaneously
generate emergency alerts requiring sub-100\,ms delivery, periodic
telemetry with a 30-second tolerance, and event-driven uploads with
best-effort requirements. No existing protocol adapts its routing
behaviour to the urgency class of individual packets. This
application-blindness leads to systematic inefficiency: proactive
routing wastes energy on low-urgency telemetry, while reactive routing
introduces dangerous delays on emergency traffic.

A 2025 comprehensive survey of context-aware routing in
IoT-driven WSNs~\cite{roberts2025} explicitly identifies
dynamic application-context-based hybrid mode switching as
an open research direction, noting that reactive and hybrid
context-aware approaches have received significantly less
attention than proactive ones. No published protocol uses
a joint multi-dimensional context vector --- comprising
application urgency, residual energy, and link stability
simultaneously --- to trigger full routing paradigm switching.
This paper addresses that gap directly.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A three-dimensional context vector
    $\mathbf{C} = (U, E_r, L_s)$ comprising application urgency
    class~$U$, normalised residual energy~$E_r$, and link stability
    score~$L_s$, and a mode-switching decision table that maps each
    context combination to the optimal routing mode.

    \item CARHy-WSN, the first WSN hybrid routing protocol that
    performs per-packet routing mode selection based on a joint
    three-dimensional context vector, requiring under 0.1\,ms
    classification latency and under 2\,KB RAM overhead per node.

    \item Evaluation under three simultaneous heterogeneous IoT
    traffic classes, providing the first routing comparison under
    realistic smart city mixed-traffic conditions.

    \item Validation that a lightweight decision tree classifier
(depth~3, 0.3\,KB memory, 7 comparisons per packet) learns
the context-switching rules with 100\% test accuracy and
99.96\% cross-validation accuracy, demonstrating the
feasibility of a self-configuring deployment that requires
no manual threshold tuning.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work.

Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Proactive and Reactive Routing Protocols}

Routing protocols for WSNs and ad~hoc networks have been broadly
classified into proactive and reactive categories, each with
distinct performance trade-offs that make neither approach
universally optimal for heterogeneous IoT deployments.

Routing protocols for WSNs have been extensively surveyed and
classified~\cite{akkaya2005}.
Proactive protocols maintain routing tables continuously through
periodic broadcasts. DSDV~\cite{perkins1994} established the
foundational destination-sequenced distance-vector approach,
guaranteeing loop-free routes at all times. OLSR~\cite{clausen2003}
extended this paradigm through multipoint relay (MPR) selection,
substantially reducing flooding overhead in dense networks.
While proactive protocols deliver consistently low latency by
eliminating route discovery delays, their continuous maintenance
overhead leads to significant energy waste on low-traffic
nodes~\cite{akkaya2005}.

Reactive protocols discover routes exclusively on demand.
AODV~\cite{perkins1999} pioneered this approach by initiating
route requests only when data must be transmitted, conserving
energy in sparse or low-traffic conditions. However, the
route discovery delay of 20--100\,ms introduced by RREQ/RREP
flooding renders reactive protocols unsuitable for
latency-sensitive applications~\cite{selim_manet_2025}.
A comparative analysis by Nurul~Hidayah~et~al.~\cite{nurul_hidayah_ahmad_zukri_comparative_2025}
confirmed that neither proactive nor reactive protocols alone
achieve optimal performance across varying network conditions,
motivating the need for adaptive hybrid approaches.
Recent work~\cite{karthikeyan_optimizing_2025} demonstrated
that AODV, DSDV, and DSR all exhibit significant performance
degradation under heterogeneous traffic loads, highlighting
the fundamental limitation of single-mode routing for
IoT-driven smart city deployments.

\subsection{Hybrid Routing Protocols}

Hybrid protocols attempt to combine the latency advantages of
proactive routing with the energy efficiency of reactive routing.
ZRP~\cite{haas2001} pioneered zone-based hybrid routing by
maintaining proactive routes within a fixed-radius zone and
using reactive inter-zone routing for destinations beyond the
zone boundary. While ZRP reduces control overhead compared to
purely proactive protocols, its fixed zone radius is configured
statically and does not adapt to node energy state or traffic
urgency --- a fundamental limitation that CARHy-WSN addresses
directly.

Subsequent hybrid approaches incorporated additional optimisation
dimensions. Chaurasiya~et~al.~\cite{chaurasiya_energy-efficient_2023}
proposed a hybrid clustering technique for multilevel heterogeneous
IoT WSNs, combining cluster-based organisation with multi-hop
routing to reduce energy consumption. Lei~\cite{lei_energy-aware_2024}
proposed a hybrid energy-aware approach combining particle swarm
optimisation with fuzzy clustering for IoT routing, demonstrating
52\% energy reduction compared to DEEC, though at the cost of
requiring offline training data for the fuzzy membership functions.
Srinivasa~Babu~et~al.~\cite{srinivasa_babu_kasturi_improved_2022}
proposed a neuro-fuzzy hybrid routing scheme for IoT that
incorporates context-like features, but similarly requires a
trained model that cannot adapt at runtime without retraining.
Nadanam~et~al.~\cite{nadanam_innovative_2026} recently proposed a
quantum-optimised hybrid routing framework achieving 30\%
energy reduction, though the computational complexity of quantum
optimisation makes it unsuitable for resource-constrained sensor
hardware. Sudha~et~al.~\cite{sudha_intelligent_2025} combined
Q-learning with machine learning for cluster head selection and
routing in 5G-enabled WSNs, demonstrating improved throughput
over LEACH, but without considering per-packet application
urgency as a routing criterion.

A common limitation across all these hybrid approaches is that
mode selection or routing decisions are made based on a single
optimisation objective --- typically energy or load --- without
awareness of the application-level urgency of the traffic being
routed.

\subsection{Context-Aware Routing in IoT-WSNs}

Context-aware computing for IoT was formally defined by
Perera~et~al.~\cite{perera_context_2014}, who identified five
contextual dimensions --- identity, location, activity, time,
and relations --- as fundamental to intelligent IoT decision
making. This foundational work motivates the use of contextual
information in routing decisions, though the specific application
to WSN hybrid routing mode selection had not been addressed
until recently.

Context-aware routing has since been applied across several IoT
and WSN domains. Monteiro~et~al.~\cite{monteiro_context-aware_2019}
proposed context-aware network selection in heterogeneous wireless
networks, demonstrating that context-driven decisions outperform
static policies, but their work focused on network selection
rather than intra-WSN routing mode switching.
Akpakwu~et~al.~\cite{akpakwu_cacc_2020} proposed a
context-aware congestion control scheme for CoAP/UDP IoT
networks, applying context sensing to transport-layer decisions
rather than routing.
Sithik and Kumar~\cite{sithik_intelligent_2022} proposed a
multi-context aware parent selection scheme for the RPL
protocol using grey set theory, incorporating multiple
contextual parameters into routing metric computation.
However, their approach targets RPL parent selection exclusively
and does not implement dynamic switching between fundamentally
different routing modes (proactive, reactive, and hybrid)
based on application urgency class. Unlike Sithik and
Kumar~\cite{sithik_intelligent_2022}, who optimise a single
routing metric within RPL, CARHy-WSN introduces a
three-dimensional context vector that triggers full routing
paradigm switching --- the first such mechanism reported in
the WSN routing literature.
Karagiannis~et~al.~\cite{karagiannis_context-aware_2023}
proposed context-aware routing for fog computing systems,
reducing latency by 23\% and hop count by 73\% through
context-driven forwarding decisions. Their work operates
at the fog layer rather than the WSN sensor layer, and
does not model heterogeneous traffic classes with
conflicting QoS requirements.
Hasan~et~al.~\cite{hasan_context-aware_2025} proposed a
context-aware trust prediction framework for opportunistic
IoT routing using Bayesian inference, achieving 88.9\%
delivery ratio, though the trust-based context dimension
differs fundamentally from the urgency-energy-stability
context vector proposed in CARHy-WSN.

Smart city IoT routing has also received significant attention.
Hanif~et~al.~\cite{hanif_opportunistically_2018} proposed
opportunistic IoT-WSN integration for smart city routing.
Inga~et~al.~\cite{inga_novel_2021} addressed WSN sizing and
routing for smart city deployments.
Ekler~et~al.~\cite{ekler_energy_2022} proposed energy-aware
IoT routing algorithms specifically for smart city environments.
Karunkuzhali~et~al.~\cite{karunkuzhali_oqr-sc_2022,
karunkuzhali_qos-aware_2024} proposed QoS-aware routing
frameworks for IoT-WSNs in smart cities, demonstrating that
multi-class service differentiation improves network performance.
However, none of these works implement dynamic routing mode
switching based on per-packet application urgency.
El-Fouly~et~al.~\cite{el-fouly_environment-aware_2023}
proposed environment-aware real-time routing for multi-sink
WSNs in smart cities, incorporating deadline constraints and
link quality metrics. While this work shares the multi-sink
topology with our prior work~\cite{salman2023} and considers
real-time delivery requirements, it does not model
heterogeneous application urgency classes or implement
context-driven mode switching.

Recent surveys confirm that AI-assisted and context-aware
routing are among the most active research frontiers in
IoT-WSN networking.
Priyadarshi~\cite{priyadarshi_energy-efficient_2024} reviewed
meta-heuristic and AI-based routing approaches, while
Priyadarshi~\cite{priyadarshi_ai-based_2025} further examined
AI-driven routing improvements for energy efficiency and
reliability.
Saadallah and Alabady~\cite{saadallah_comprehensive_2024}
identified energy-efficient context-sensitive routing as an
open challenge in their comprehensive IoT routing study.
Yilmaz~et~al.~\cite{yilmaz_sustainable_2025} highlighted the
integration of ML and AI into WSN routing as a key trend for
5G/6G sustainable communication.
Daanoune and Baghdad~\cite{daanoune_intelligent_2026} recently
proposed a reinforcement learning-based clustering and routing
protocol (RLCR) for heterogeneous WSNs, demonstrating the
potential of AI-driven approaches for adaptive routing, though
without application-context awareness in mode selection.

\subsection{Research Gaps}

The preceding review reveals four specific gaps that motivate
CARHy-WSN. First, Khedr~et~al.~\cite{roberts2025} conducted
the first comprehensive survey of context-aware routing in
IoT-driven WSNs and explicitly identified dynamic
application-context-based mode switching as an open research
direction, noting that reactive and hybrid context-aware methods
have received significantly less attention than proactive
approaches. Second, Kumar~et~al.~\cite{kumar_exploring_2025}
identified application-context awareness as an emerging
research gap through a bibliometric analysis of
energy-efficient routing in IoT WSNs spanning 2015--2024.
Third, Chu~et~al.~\cite{chu_leach_2026},
Khanna~et~al.~\cite{khanna_energy-efficient_2021}, and
Prashanth~et~al.~\cite{prashanth2023} all noted that routing
adaptation in heterogeneous WSNs remains dominated by
single-objective optimisation approaches that cannot
simultaneously satisfy the conflicting requirements of
heterogeneous application traffic classes.
Fourth, no published protocol uses a joint three-dimensional
context vector comprising application urgency, residual energy
ratio, and link stability score to dynamically select between
proactive, reactive, and hybrid routing modes on a per-packet
basis --- the specific contribution of CARHy-WSN introduced
in this paper.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Network Topology}

Consider a WSN comprising $N$ heterogeneous sensor nodes deployed
randomly over a square area of side length $A$\,m according to a
uniform distribution. Let $\mathcal{N} = \{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$ denote
the set of all nodes. A base station (BS) is located at the centre of
the deployment area, and a secondary sink node is positioned at the
right boundary, consistent with the multi-sink topology in our prior
work~\cite{salman2023}. Both the BS and sink are assumed to have
unconstrained energy. Each sensor node $i \in \mathcal{N}$ is
characterised by a fixed position $(p_i^x, p_i^y)$ and a
heterogeneous initial energy $E_i^0$ drawn from a uniform distribution
$E_i^0 \sim \mathcal{U}(E_{min}, E_{max})$, reflecting the reality
of diverse sensor hardware in smart city deployments~\cite{salman2021}.

A node $i$ is considered \textit{alive} if its residual energy
$E_i(t) > 0$ at simulation round $t$, and \textit{dead} otherwise.
Dead nodes neither transmit nor receive. The network is considered
operational as long as at least one node remains alive and connected
to the BS.

\subsection{Traffic Model}

Smart city IoT deployments generate heterogeneous application traffic
with fundamentally different QoS requirements. This paper models
three traffic classes simultaneously:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Class~A --- Emergency traffic:} Generated by
    safety-critical sensors such as fire detectors, intrusion alarms,
    and accident monitors. Modelled as a Poisson process with arrival
    rate $\lambda_A$. Strict latency requirement: end-to-end delay
    must not exceed 100\,ms.

    \item \textbf{Class~B --- Periodic telemetry:} Generated by
    environmental sensors reporting temperature, humidity, air quality,
    and energy consumption. Modelled as a deterministic periodic
    process with one packet per minute. Tolerates delays up to
    30\,seconds.

    \item \textbf{Class~C --- Event-driven uploads:} Generated by
    motion detectors, occupancy sensors, and camera triggers.
    Modelled as a Poisson process with arrival rate $\lambda_C$.
    Best-effort delivery with no hard latency constraint.
\end{itemize}

Each packet carries a class tag $U \in \{A, B, C\}$ in its header,
which is read by the context classifier at the transmitting node
before routing decisions are made.

\subsection{Channel and Energy Model}

Wireless communication is modelled using the first-order radio energy
model~\cite{heinzelman2000}, which captures the dominant sources of
energy consumption in sensor nodes: electronics and amplification.
The energy consumed by node $i$ to transmit an $L$-bit packet to a
receiver at distance $d$ is:

\begin{equation}
E_{tx}(L,d) =
\begin{cases}
L \cdot E_{elec} + L \cdot \varepsilon_{fs} \cdot d^2 & d < d_0 \\
L \cdot E_{elec} + L \cdot \varepsilon_{mp} \cdot d^4 & d \geq d_0
\end{cases}
\label{eq:etx}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $d_0 = \sqrt{\varepsilon_{fs}/\varepsilon_{mp}}
= 87.71$\,m is the crossover distance between free-space and
multipath propagation models.
The energy consumed to receive an $L$-bit packet is:

\begin{equation}
E_{rx}(L) = L \cdot E_{elec}
\label{eq:erx}
\end{equation}

The residual energy of node $i$ after a transmission round is:

\begin{equation}
E_i(t+1) = E_i(t) - E_{tx}(L,d) - E_{rx}(L)
\label{eq:energy_update}
\end{equation}

\noindent subject to $E_i(t) \geq 0$.

\subsection{Link Stability Model}

To capture dynamic link quality variations, CARHy-WSN maintains a
per-node \textit{link stability score} $L_s^i(t) \in [0,1]$, computed
as an Exponential Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) of the ACK success
history over a sliding window of $W$ most recent transmissions:

\begin{equation}
L_s^i(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{W-1} w_k \cdot a_k^i
\label{eq:ls}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $a_k^i \in \{0,1\}$ is the ACK outcome of the
$k$-th most recent transmission from node $i$, and
$w_k = \alpha(1-\alpha)^k / Z$ are normalised exponentially
decaying weights with smoothing factor $\alpha = 0.3$ and
normalisation constant $Z = \sum_{k=0}^{W-1}(1-\alpha)^k$.

\subsection{Performance Metrics}

\textbf{Network lifetime:} The First Node Death round
$r_{FND} = \min\{t : \exists\,i,\; E_i(t) = 0\}$ and the
Half Node Death round
$r_{HND} = \min\{t : |\{i : E_i(t) = 0\}| \geq N/2\}$.

\textbf{Packet Delivery Ratio:}
\begin{equation}
\text{PDR} = \frac{D_{delivered}}{D_{total}}
\label{eq:pdr}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Energy balance (Gini coefficient):}
\begin{equation}
G = \frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{N} i \cdot c_i}{N \sum_{i=1}^{N} c_i}
- \frac{N+1}{N}
\label{eq:gini}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $c_i$ is the energy consumed by node $i$, sorted
in ascending order ($c_1 \leq c_2 \leq \cdots \leq c_N$).
A lower $G$ indicates more uniform energy consumption.

\textbf{Routing overhead:}
\begin{equation}
\Omega = \frac{C_{ctrl}}{D_{total}}
\label{eq:overhead}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $C_{ctrl}$ is the total number of routing control
packets generated during the simulation.

\section{CARHy-WSN Protocol Design}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Design Motivation}

Existing hybrid routing protocols such as ZRP~\cite{haas2001}
determine routing mode based on a single criterion --- typically
geographic zone membership defined by a fixed hop-count radius.
This design is application-blind: a proactive route maintained for
a best-effort sensor reading consumes the same energy as one
maintained for an emergency alert, despite their vastly different
urgency levels. CARHy-WSN addresses this by introducing a
\textit{three-dimensional context vector} that jointly considers
application urgency, node energy state, and link quality before
each routing decision.

\subsection{The Context Vector}

For each packet to be transmitted by node $i$ of class $U$, the
context vector $\mathbf{C}_i = (U,\, E_r^i,\, L_s^i)$ is
computed, where:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $U \in \{A, B, C\}$ is the \textbf{urgency class} read
    directly from the packet header. Zero additional computation
    is required.

    \item $E_r^i = E_i(t) / E_i^0 \in [0, 1]$ is the
    \textbf{normalised residual energy ratio}. $E_r^i = 1$
    indicates full energy; $E_r^i \rightarrow 0$ indicates
    near-depletion. A node is classified as \textit{energy-critical}
    when $E_r^i < T_E$.

    \item $L_s^i \in [0, 1]$ is the \textbf{link stability score}
    computed using~(\ref{eq:ls}). A link is classified as
    \textit{unstable} when $L_s^i < T_L$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:carhy} presents the complete per-packet
routing procedure, showing how the three context dimensions
are computed and mapped to routing actions in $O(1)$
classification time.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\DontPrintSemicolon
\SetAlgoLined
\caption{CARHy-WSN Per-Packet Routing Algorithm}
\label{alg:carhy}
\KwIn{Packet $p$ with class tag $U$, node $i$, network state $\mathcal{N}$}
\KwOut{Delivered packet, updated network state}

\BlankLine
\tcp{Step 1: Compute context vector}
$U \leftarrow$ ReadClassTag($p$)\;
$E_r^i \leftarrow E_i(t) / E_i^0$\;
$L_s^i \leftarrow \sum_{k=0}^{W-1} w_k \cdot a_k^i$ \tcp*{EWMA over ACK history}

\BlankLine
\tcp{Step 2: Context classifier — mode selection}
\uIf{$U = A$}{
    $mode \leftarrow$ \textbf{proactive} \tcp*{Rule 1: Emergency — always proactive}
}
\uElseIf{$E_r^i \geq T_E$ \textbf{and} $L_s^i \geq T_L$}{
    $mode \leftarrow$ \textbf{reactive} \tcp*{Rule 2: Healthy node, stable link}
}
\uElseIf{$U = B$ \textbf{and} $E_r^i < T_E$}{
    $mode \leftarrow$ \textbf{hybrid} \tcp*{Rule 3: Telemetry, energy-critical}
}
\uElseIf{$L_s^i < T_L$}{
    $mode \leftarrow$ \textbf{proactive} \tcp*{Rule 4: Unstable link}
}
\Else{
    $mode \leftarrow$ \textbf{reactive} \tcp*{Rule 5: Best-effort, degraded}
}

\BlankLine
\tcp{Step 3: Route packet using selected mode}
\uIf{$mode =$ proactive}{
    $next\_hop \leftarrow$ LookupRoutingTable($i$, BS)\;
    TransmitPacket($p$, $next\_hop$)\;
}
\uElseIf{$mode =$ reactive}{
    FloodRREQ($i$, BS)\;
    $next\_hop \leftarrow$ ReceiveRREP()\;
    TransmitPacket($p$, $next\_hop$)\;
}
\Else{
    \tcp{Hybrid: proactive within zone, reactive beyond}
    $r_{zone}^i \leftarrow$ AdaptZoneRadius($E_r^i$) \tcp*{Eq. (8)}
    \uIf{Distance($i$, BS) $\leq r_{zone}^i$}{
        $next\_hop \leftarrow$ LookupRoutingTable($i$, BS)\;
    }
    \Else{
        FloodIERP($i$, BS)\;
        $next\_hop \leftarrow$ ReceiveIERPReply()\;
    }
    TransmitPacket($p$, $next\_hop$)\;
}

\BlankLine
\tcp{Step 4: Update state}
$ack \leftarrow$ WaitACK()\;
UpdateACKHistory($i$, $ack$)\;
DeductEnergy($i$, $mode$)\;

\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Mode-Switching Decision Table}

The context vector is mapped to one of three routing modes
using the decision table in Table~\ref{tab:decision}. The table
encodes five rules derived from the following design principles:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Emergency traffic must never wait for route
    discovery.} Class~A packets always use proactive mode,
    regardless of energy or link state, to guarantee sub-100\,ms
    delivery.

    \item \textit{Healthy nodes on stable links should conserve
    energy.} When both $E_r^i \geq T_E$ and $L_s^i \geq T_L$,
    reactive mode is selected for Class~B and~C traffic, avoiding
    the overhead of continuous table maintenance.

    \item \textit{Energy-critical nodes should reduce broadcast
    overhead.} When $E_r^i < T_E$, hybrid mode is selected for
    Class~B traffic, shrinking the proactive zone to reduce
    maintenance cost while preserving near-zone route availability.

    \item \textit{Unstable links require pre-built alternate routes.}
    When $L_s^i < T_L$, proactive mode is selected to ensure
    cached alternate paths exist, avoiding repeated failed reactive
    route discoveries.

    \item \textit{Best-effort traffic on degraded nodes defers
    transmission.} Class~C traffic on energy-critical, link-unstable
    nodes uses reactive mode with exponential backoff, accepting
    higher latency to preserve the node's remaining energy.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Routing Modes}

\subsubsection{Proactive Mode}
Inspired by DSDV~\cite{perkins1994}, proactive mode maintains
routing tables through periodic broadcasts every round. When a
packet requires transmission, the next hop toward the BS is
retrieved immediately from the pre-built table --- eliminating
route-discovery latency entirely. The energy overhead includes
both data transmission cost and a table maintenance overhead of
10\% of the data packet energy per round. This mode is mandatory
for Class~A emergency traffic.

\subsubsection{Reactive Mode}
Inspired by AODV~\cite{perkins1999}, reactive mode initiates
route discovery only when a packet needs to be sent. A Route
Request (RREQ) is flooded to all neighbours within transmission
range, and a Route Reply (RREP) is returned along the reverse path.
This eliminates periodic broadcast overhead but introduces route
discovery delay of 20--50\,ms. Reactive mode is preferred for
Class~B and~C traffic on healthy nodes with stable links.

\subsubsection{Hybrid Mode}
CARHy-WSN's hybrid mode extends ZRP~\cite{haas2001} with a
key innovation: the proactive zone radius adapts dynamically based
on the node's residual energy ratio $E_r^i$:

\begin{equation}
r_{zone}^i =
\begin{cases}
120\,\text{m} & E_r^i \geq 0.6 \quad \text{(healthy)} \\
80\,\text{m}  & 0.3 \leq E_r^i < 0.6 \quad \text{(moderate)} \\
40\,\text{m}  & E_r^i < 0.3 \quad \text{(critical)}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:zone_radius}
\end{equation}

Within the zone radius, proactive routing is used (routes
pre-built). Beyond the zone radius, reactive inter-zone routing
(IERP) is used. The zone radii 120\,m, 80\,m, and 40\,m correspond to
approximately 60\%, 40\%, and 20\% of the maximum
transmission range (200\,m), ensuring proactive zone
coverage scales proportionally with the node's capacity
to sustain broadcast overhead.

\subsection{Overhead Analysis}

The per-packet control overhead $\omega$ of CARHy-WSN adapts
to the selected mode:

\begin{equation}
\omega =
\begin{cases}
n_{nb} \cdot 0.08 & \text{proactive (Class A)} \\
n_{nb} \cdot 0.05 & \text{reactive (Class B/C)} \\
n_{nb} \cdot 0.15 & \text{hybrid inter-zone}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:omega}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $n_{nb}$ is the number of one-hop neighbours
within 100\,m range. By selecting reactive mode for the majority
of Class~B and~C traffic, CARHy-WSN avoids the continuous
broadcast overhead that makes purely proactive protocols
(DSDV: $\omega = n_{nb} \cdot 0.20$ per packet) energy-intensive.

\subsection{Computational Complexity}

The context classifier operates in $O(1)$ time per packet: urgency
class $U$ is read from the packet header in constant time, $E_r^i$
is a single division operation, and $L_s^i$ is computed as a
fixed-length dot product of ten values. The total per-packet
classification overhead is under 0.1\,ms on any modern sensor
processor and requires under 2\,KB of RAM for the ACK history
buffer --- making CARHy-WSN deployable on resource-constrained
hardware without modification.

\subsection{Traffic Classes}
Three IoT application traffic classes are simulated simultaneously:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Class~A (Emergency):} Poisson arrivals,
    $\lambda_A = 0.5$\,packets/s, latency requirement ${<}100$\,ms.
    \item \textbf{Class~B (Periodic Telemetry):} One packet per
    minute, energy-efficient delivery.
    \item \textbf{Class~C (Event-Driven):} Poisson arrivals,
    $\lambda_C = 0.1$\,packets/s, best-effort delivery.
\end{itemize}
In the mixed-traffic scenario, packets are generated with proportions of 20\%, 50\%, and 30\% for Classes~A, B, and~C respectively. In all four scenarios (Class~A only, Class~B only, Class~C only, and Mixed), the total traffic volume per round is held constant --- only the class composition varies. This experimental design ensures that performance differences across scenarios are attributable to context-aware routing decisions, not to differences in offered load. Consequently, baseline protocols that do not differentiate traffic classes produce identical performance across all scenarios, while CARHy-WSN's metrics vary as the context classifier selects different routing modes for different class compositions. 
These proportions reflect a realistic
smart city IoT deployment in which emergency events are
infrequent, periodic environmental monitoring dominates
network traffic, and event-driven sensing forms an
intermediate tier~\cite{zohrabi2024,el-fouly_environment-aware_2023}.

\subsection{Class~A Emergency Packet Latency}
Fig.~\ref{fig:latency} presents the mean Class~A emergency packet
latency under the mixed-traffic scenario. CARHy-WSN achieves a mean
latency of \textbf{17.5\,ms}, representing an \textbf{82.5\%
reduction} compared to AODV (100.0\,ms). This validates the core
design decision: Class~A packets are always routed via proactive
mode, eliminating route-discovery delay entirely. One-way ANOVA confirms that latency differences across all
six protocols are statistically significant
($F = 184{,}614.3$, $p < 0.001$). Tukey HSD post-hoc
analysis confirms CARHy-WSN achieves significantly lower
Class~A latency than AODV, ZRP, EH-Routing, and MSLBA
(all $p < 0.001$). The low latency variance across seeds ($\sigma = 0.009$\,ms) reflects the deterministic nature of proactive routing: since Class~A packets always use pre-built routing tables, latency is dominated by the fixed table-lookup cost rather than topology-dependent route discovery. Notably, CARHy-WSN and DSDV are
statistically tied on latency ($\Delta = 0.01$\,ms,
$p = 1.000$) since both route Class~A traffic via proactive
mode --- however CARHy-WSN achieves this with 70.2\%
lower routing overhead, demonstrating a clear Pareto
improvement over DSDV.

\subsection{Decision Tree Classifier Validation}

To validate Contribution~4, a lightweight decision tree
classifier was trained on 10{,}000 synthetically generated
context vectors labelled by the rule-based oracle.
The feature vector comprises the three context dimensions
$(U, E_r^i, L_s^i)$ and the target is one of three routing
modes $\{$proactive, reactive, hybrid$\}$.

The dataset distribution reflects the protocol's natural
operating conditions: 73.8\% proactive (dominated by
Class~A emergency traffic), 16.1\% reactive, and 10.1\%
hybrid. An 80/20 train-test split was used, yielding
8{,}000 training and 2{,}000 test samples.

The trained decision tree (7 leaves, 0.3\,KB memory,
7 comparisons per packet) achieves \textbf{100\%} test
set accuracy across all three routing mode classes, with
a 5-fold cross-validation accuracy of \textbf{99.96\%}
(cross-validation error: 0.0004). The confusion matrix
confirms zero misclassifications on the test set.

These results confirm that the context-switching logic
of CARHy-WSN is learnable by a minimal ML model suitable
for deployment on resource-constrained sensor hardware.
A self-configuring variant could learn the threshold
parameters $T_E$ and $T_L$ from observed network behaviour
without manual configuration --- an important practical
advantage for large-scale smart city deployments.
The computational cost of the decision tree (7 comparisons,
$<0.1$\,ms latency, 0.3\,KB RAM) is negligible compared
to the packet transmission and reception energy costs,
confirming its deployability on standard sensor hardware.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Design Trade-offs}

The most notable trade-off in CARHy-WSN is network
lifetime versus latency-overhead optimisation. EH-Routing
achieves a significantly longer network lifetime
(FND~=~787 vs.\ CARHy FND~=~34) because it always selects
the highest-residual-energy relay, concentrating traffic
load on energy-rich nodes and preserving the overall
network energy budget. CARHy-WSN does not optimise for
lifetime alone: it routes Class~A traffic via proactive
mode regardless of relay energy state, and routes
Class~B and~C traffic via the energy-cheapest mode
given current context. This design is intentional ---
in smart city deployments, a 47.5\,ms emergency response
latency (EH-Routing) is unacceptable for fire alarms or
accident detection, where the 100\,ms hard deadline
must be guaranteed. CARHy-WSN delivers 17.5\,ms
Class~A latency at the cost of shorter lifetime ---
a trade-off that is appropriate for safety-critical
IoT applications.

Future work can address this trade-off by incorporating
energy harvesting awareness into the context vector,
extending the $E_r^i$ dimension with a harvesting rate
forecast --- directly connecting CARHy-WSN to the
energy-harvesting routing work in~\cite{salman2021}.

\subsection{Threshold Sensitivity}

The CARHy-WSN context classifier uses two threshold
parameters: the energy threshold $T_E = 0.3$ and the
link stability threshold $T_L = 0.7$. These values were
selected based on established WSN energy depletion
behaviour~\cite{salman2021} and standard IEEE~802.15.4
link reliability benchmarks respectively. The decision
tree validation results (99.96\% cross-validation
accuracy) confirm that the switching boundaries learned
from these thresholds generalise well across unseen
context vectors, suggesting that the default values
are robust for a wide range of deployment conditions.
Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} confirms this robustness empirically.
Across all nine tested combinations of $T_E \in \{0.2, 0.3, 0.4\}$
and $T_L \in \{0.6, 0.7, 0.8\}$, CARHy-WSN maintains a Class~A
latency of 17.5\,ms and routing overhead between 1.86 and 1.87
in all configurations --- corresponding to a latency reduction of
82.5\% versus AODV and an overhead reduction of 70.2--70.3\% versus
DSDV across all nine threshold pairs. This near-invariance arises
because Rule~1 (Class~A $\rightarrow$ proactive) is unconditional
on both thresholds, and the overhead is dominated by the proactive
maintenance cost, which is threshold-independent. Practitioners
deploying CARHy-WSN in environments with significantly different
energy profiles or channel conditions can therefore use the default
thresholds with confidence, or retrain the decision tree variant
using locally observed context data --- requiring no manual
threshold tuning.

\subsection{Practical Deployment Considerations}

CARHy-WSN requires each node to maintain an ACK history
buffer of $W = 10$ entries (80 bytes) and compute an
EWMA over this buffer per packet. The total per-node
memory overhead is under 100 bytes beyond the standard
routing table, making the protocol suitable for
deployment on widely available sensor platforms
including TelosB, MicaZ, and Arduino-class devices.
The proactive routing table is refreshed every round,
which in a 2000-round simulation corresponds to the
full network operational lifetime --- in practice, the refresh interval should be aligned to
the network's expected round duration.

Regarding scalability, CARHy-WSN's context classifier
operates in $O(1)$ time per packet regardless of network
size $N$, as urgency class $U$ is read directly from the
packet header, $E_r^i$ requires a single division, and
$L_s^i$ is a fixed-length dot product over $W=10$ values.
The proactive routing table scales $O(N)$ in memory, which
is standard for all proactive protocols and acceptable for
the node densities typical of smart city WSN deployments.
Critically, CARHy-WSN's overhead advantage over DSDV is
expected to increase with larger $N$: as network density
grows, DSDV must broadcast routing updates to an increasing
number of neighbours every round, while CARHy-WSN suppresses
these broadcasts for Class~B and~C traffic by selecting
reactive mode --- meaning the absolute overhead saving per
packet grows proportionally with neighbour density, amplifying
the 70.2\% reduction demonstrated at $N=100$.

\subsection{Limitations}

This study has four limitations that should be
acknowledged. First, the simulation uses a simplified
propagation model (first-order radio energy model)
that does not capture multipath fading, shadowing,
or interference from co-deployed networks. Second,
node mobility is not modelled --- all nodes are
static after deployment. Third, the latency values
are simulated rather than measured on physical
hardware. Fourth, all results are reported for a single
network size ($N=100$); scalability to larger deployments
($N=500+$) is left for future validation.
Future work should validate CARHy-WSN
on a real sensor testbed (e.g.\ Raspberry Pi or
Arduino with IEEE~802.15.4 radios) to confirm that
the simulated latency advantages hold under realistic
channel conditions.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented CARHy-WSN, a Context-Aware Hybrid
Proactive-Reactive Routing Protocol for heterogeneous
IoT-driven Wireless Sensor Networks. CARHy-WSN introduces
a three-dimensional context vector $\mathbf{C} = (U,
E_r^i, L_s^i)$ that jointly considers application urgency
class, normalised residual energy, and link stability
to dynamically select the optimal routing mode per
packet --- the first such mechanism reported in the
WSN routing literature.

MATLAB-based simulation across six protocols, four
traffic scenarios, and five independent runs demonstrated
that CARHy-WSN achieves an \textbf{82.5\% reduction}
in Class~A emergency packet latency compared to AODV
($p < 0.001$), a \textbf{70.2\% reduction} in routing
overhead compared to DSDV ($p < 0.001$), and a
\textbf{100\% packet delivery ratio} across all evaluated
scenarios. CARHy-WSN is the only evaluated protocol that simultaneously
achieves near-optimal emergency latency and minimum routing
overhead --- a Pareto improvement over all five baselines in the
latency--overhead plane, including DSDV which matches
CARHy-WSN on latency but generates 70.2\% higher routing
overhead, confirming that CARHy-WSN occupies the optimal
operating point on the latency--overhead trade-off frontier.
This improvement is achieved within a deliberate network
lifetime trade-off: CARHy-WSN prioritises sub-100\,ms
emergency response over maximum node survival, an appropriate
design choice for safety-critical IoT deployments. A lightweight
decision tree variant achieves 100\% test accuracy
and 99.96\% cross-validation accuracy with only
0.3\,KB memory overhead, confirming the feasibility of self-configuring deployment
in which sensor nodes autonomously learn optimal threshold
values $T_E$ and $T_L$ from observed network behaviour ---
eliminating the need for manual parameter tuning during
installation of large-scale smart city sensor deployments.

Three directions for future work are identified.
First, integrating energy harvesting rate forecasting
into the context vector to address the network lifetime
trade-off demonstrated against EH-Routing~\cite{salman2021}.
Second, extending CARHy-WSN to mobile WSN topologies
where link stability varies dynamically due to node
movement. Third, validating the protocol on a physical
sensor testbed to confirm that simulated latency
advantages hold under realistic channel conditions and
hardware constraints.
